Where the powers come from

An origin story is an argument about causation. It tells us what a person can do, where that ability came from, and what had to change before it became visible. Putting someone in a cape makes this argument unusually easy to see. The cape also makes it possible to say things about ourselves that would be difficult to say with a straight face.

That is part of what we have been exploring in the AI and storytelling workshops this week. I began making superhero stories about myself and then about Jonah, Madeleine, and Jordan, some of the people helping lead those workshops. These four are a small cast for an experiment, not a complete portrait of the Learning Lab or the people whose work makes it possible. Giving each person an origin, and then bringing them together, offered a way to ask what different histories contribute to a shared project.

My first story began on the planet of writing teachers. On that planet, people routinely helped someone find the shape of an argument, recognize a pattern across unfamiliar materials, or turn a head full of ideas into a project they could understand and feel proud of. Those were ordinary abilities there. In another setting, among code, cameras, and complicated academic projects, the same abilities could look extraordinary. The rocket leaving the planet was ridiculous. The account of the work was sincere.

The metaphor becomes more interesting when its inconveniences arrive. Jonah never left Expos, Harvard's writing program. His appointment spans Expos and writing pedagogy at the Bok Center. If he can still come from that world, the planet cannot simply have exploded. His story changes mine. It introduces a surviving place, an ongoing craft, and a relationship between worlds that the first escape story had made too simple. A good collaborator can make your origin story less flattering, less complete, and more accurate.

Madeleine's experience with folklore, mythology, botanical traditions, catalogs, and indexes suggests a different passage. Those habits of mind can inform how she builds research helpers, interactive essays, and other academic projects with AI. The interesting claim is about the imaginative character of those systems: the meaningful relations she notices and the experiences she wants a reader to have. Turning a grimoire into a context pack makes that claim visible. It need not imply that software engineers lack imagination or that folklore is valuable only when it can be converted into software.

Jordan's story gives color a similarly literal force. Her background in arts and education, together with a deliberately mythical island of horses, farms, mountains, and sea, becomes the starting point for a character entering an academic world that can treat “that's fun” as a dismissal. Making color her power asks what gets excluded when intellectual seriousness is imagined as gray. The joke changes when the same words become an invitation to make something together.

These stories are playful enough to resist becoming institutional testimonials. That resistance matters. A superhero portrait can very quickly become a machine for congratulating its subject. I want the joke to remain alive even when the ability being described is real. The shared workshop is more interesting than a collection of exceptional people announcing their exceptional qualities.

A small form with several jobs

The comic grew out of a larger interest in microstories: the little three-to-five-minute accounts through which we explain what happened, why it matters, and what we now want to investigate. In the Story Lab, we are trying to get better at finding those stories in the week's work. A usable record requires more than an inventory of activities. Someone who was not there needs a setting, an event or change, and enough relation between things to understand the significance.

The time limit is a working constraint, not a universal law of attention. Three to five minutes often gives us room for a frame, a couple of concrete examples, and a thought that joins them. Eight comic panels offer a related opportunity. Four panels make another demand: fewer transitions, greater pressure on each image, and less room to repair something the opening failed to establish. The question becomes what the audience must know before the next thing can mean anything.

Our early generations demonstrated that problem very clearly. Some images looked accomplished while the story omitted the explanation a reader needed. A writing-program planet did not become intelligible merely because the generation instructions described one. The words the reader could actually see had to name the setting and establish what people did there. The rectangular narration box turned out to be a useful tool. It let us provide background without forcing a character to speak an implausibly compressed explanation of their life.

That is a lesson about making meaning in a medium. It is also a lesson about AI. A fact can be present somewhere in a long conversation and absent from the artifact. A caption can sound like comic-book prose while explaining almost nothing. An agent can invent a plausible reason for a professional transition and thereby change the story's central claim. Attractive images do not make those problems less consequential; they can make them harder to notice.

Starting with stories about ourselves gives participants a place from which to exercise judgment. They already know much of the relevant material. They can recognize a wrong event, an imposed motivation, or a tone they would not authorize. They can practice saying that an output is fluent but false, handsome but empty, or nearly right in a way that makes the remaining mistake especially revealing.

Even here, expertise does not guarantee immunity. The more skillfully we help a model produce convincing work, the more convincing its inventions may become. Good photographs, a consistent character sheet, and a well-developed visual style anchor likeness and continuity. They can also give an inaccurate story the appearance of authority. The person reviewing it still has to return to the actual experience, the recorded conversation, the notes, or the source material. Grounding is a practice of periodically touching something the generation did not produce.

The context pack and the script support that practice. They preserve source accounts, decisions, open questions, and corrections so that a new generation does not require starting from a vague recollection. Writing the captions before generating the page makes the proposed account available for inspection. Our failures also showed that “text first” is only a sequence until someone reads the text critically. The text needs to be checked against the central claims of the story, and the final image needs to be checked against the text.

This gives oral reflection a particular role in the workshop. A participant can explain why a scene exists, what the tool misunderstood, what they changed, and what remains unresolved. That explanation makes their judgment available to other people. The artifact and the account of making it reveal different kinds of knowledge. A beautiful comic cannot substitute for understanding the argument it makes.

Change the origin, change the explanation

Once the context was sufficiently developed, we could try different kinds of superhero origin. This is where the activity became more than a demonstration of visual styles. Changing the genre changed the answer to the question of where the power came from.

In the elsewhere story, an ability belongs to a previous world. A new environment makes it remarkable. This is the logic that made the writing-program planet useful: an account of formation, displacement, and changed conditions of recognition. The yellow sun in my version also stands for resources. The newer world is not automatically wiser or more virtuous. It has money and opportunities that change what can be done and what gets noticed. The fantasy makes an institutional condition into a physical force.

The exposure story offers a different causal account. An accident, experiment, or extraordinary encounter changes the person. In our classic cosmic-comic variations, impossible instruments turn arguments and catalogs into visible structures. The newly acquired supernatural power must be distinguished from the knowledge the character possessed before the event. Otherwise the experiment is merely decorative. This frame asks what an encounter with a new technology actually changes, rather than assuming either that it changes everything or that it only reveals what was already there.

The mutant story makes the ability innate within its fictional world. The person is already different; a crisis reveals the difference or makes hiding it impossible. Its central questions concern belonging, recognition, suppression, and the places people build for one another. That can make a school or workshop a very different kind of institution from a laboratory that produces powerful individuals. It can also become an excessively flattering account of one's own eccentricity. The metaphor needs scrutiny precisely because it feels so available.

These are interpretive possibilities, not interchangeable explanations of the actual people in the comics. A radiation accident is not an allegation about someone's biography. A mutant story is not a diagnosis, nor does making academic difference into a superhero narrative establish an equivalence with histories of racial or sexual oppression. The forms let us try accounts of a life and ask what each makes prominent, what it obscures, and where its emotional appeal outruns its accuracy.

They also invite historical questions. Superman appeared in 1938, created by Jerry Siegel and Joe Shuster, both sons of Jewish immigrants. Shuster was also an immigrant himself: born in Toronto, he moved to Cleveland as a child. The immigrant interpretation is grounded in that history, even though biography cannot determine every meaning a character will acquire. The Library of Congress traces these histories.

The Fantastic Four’s 1961 cosmic-ray accident belongs to a different imaginative setting: rockets, the space race, and the promise and danger of scientific power. Their transformation gives technological exposure a bodily consequence. This is a useful way to read the Atomic Age imagination, rather than a claim that every early Marvel story means the same thing. Marvel’s account of the Fantastic Four’s origin places their experiment in the space-race setting.

X-Men began in 1963, with an international relaunch in 1975 and further transformations through the following decades. The 1990s reference in our collection names a later visual and cultural incarnation. Marvel’s anniversary history provides that chronology. Ramzi Fawaz’s The New Mutants connects superhero transformations and alternative forms of belonging with civil rights, feminism, the New Left, and gay liberation. His argument ranges beyond the X-Men to other ensembles; the political possibilities belong to a changing field of stories and readers. NYU Press’s description of Fawaz’s study offers a starting point for that reading. These forms are historical without being reducible to a single allegory fully intended at their beginning.

A fourth frame completes this experiment: the resources story, the Batman or Iron Man version in which the apparent superpower is access to equipment, money, and the determination to keep building. It is an especially useful experiment for a lab. The joke brings budgets, purchasing, institutional support, and unequal access back into a story that could otherwise attribute every achievement to personal brilliance.

The contemporary prominence of technologically equipped wealthy heroes in major Hollywood franchises makes that frame worth examining. Batman dates to 1939 and Iron Man to 1963, so the wealthy equipment-based hero is much older than the blockbuster cycle. Nolan’s Batman films began in 2005; Iron Man opened Marvel Studios’ self-produced film sequence in 2008. Kevin Feige’s 2011 investor presentation explicitly describes its ending as announcing the interconnected Avengers universe. (DC on Batman, Marvel’s Iron Man debut issue, DC’s film chronology, Feige’s presentation.) It does not establish that the resources hero began recently, that all contemporary audiences want the same thing, or that one genre neatly replaced another. The interesting question is what gets emphasized when older fantasies are selected, enlarged, and circulated at extraordinary scale. Our extravagantly well-equipped cave can hold that question without pretending to settle the history of Hollywood or capitalism in eight panels.

The sequence matters pedagogically. I had collected comics as a child and brought an academic interest in cultural history to the exercise. Making the stories gave that knowledge a new occasion. Conversations with AI prompted questions, recollections, and possible connections that called for further reading and verification. The work became a reason to learn more about the forms we were using. We were learning while making, and changing what we made as we learned.

What the workshop is for

There are many reasons to design an activity this way. Personal material can give strangers something approachable to discuss. Small groups change who gets to speak. An interview can produce context useful in a later session. A generated image can become material for a performance. Explaining one's revisions can make otherwise invisible judgment visible. A playful form can open an argument about authenticity that an abstract debate would leave untouched.

Those reasons do not reduce to one learning objective. Their coexistence is part of the design. A successful class can have the density of a well-made scene: several things happen at once, different participants find different entrances, and details acquire significance that the designer did not fully anticipate. Thinking in terms of alignment and harmony helps us describe that density without treating every activity as a delivery mechanism for a single assessable result.

Backward design remains useful for tactical questions. If there is a particular operation students need to practice, we should think carefully about what would count as evidence that they can do it. The problem arises when the template claims to exhaust the educational undertaking. Teachers often recognize possibilities in a text, an encounter, or a practice that they cannot enumerate in advance. Their inability to produce a complete list need not mean the possibilities are unreal.

One of my strongest intuitions about teaching comes from precisely that experience: “A lot of my favorite instructors had something approaching a faith commitment that teaching King Lear was going to yield something important for students. Most of my professional adult life is in knowing that the party line is to kind of make fun of that sort of character — but deep down inside, I always sort of knew that they were right in some important way I couldn't quite name.”

That intuition does not release teachers from responsibility. It asks for a richer account of responsibility, one that includes attending to what emerges, recognizing a promising difficulty, and changing the design when the people in the room reveal something its designer did not know. Some academic expertise works like a practiced movement: responding at the right moment, hearing a hesitation, noticing that a project needs a different kind of question. A written description of that expertise is useful without being identical to possessing it.

AI makes this distinction urgent because it can produce the language of a well-aligned course so readily. A persuasive set of objectives, activities, and assessments can be generated before anyone has met the students. That can help with planning. It also makes it easier to confuse a convincing representation of an educational relationship with the relationship itself. The human work includes sustaining attention through time, negotiating trust, and helping people become capable of things whose importance they may only gradually discover.

The origin-story exercise belongs to that larger identity project. Students are considering what they care about, what they can authorize, and how they want their work to appear to other people. Genre already complicates those questions. A self does not arrive fully expressed and then get poured without resistance into a song, essay, interview, or comic. Each form supplies expectations and rewards certain ways of becoming legible. AI adds another participant, particularly capable of producing familiar patterns. The resulting tension deserves investigation throughout a course, rather than a quick verdict about whether generated work is authentic.

Historicizing our educational forms can help with that investigation, provided we do not mistake history for exposure of a fraud. A seminar paper or a close reading can be a remarkable achievement even though the practices that make it possible developed under particular material and institutional conditions. Showing that something was made does not prove that it was empty. It lets us appreciate the work through which a community built a form stable enough for people to think with.

We need that generosity toward the past together with a willingness to change. AI enters an already complicated world of teaching, research, ambition, money, care, compromise, and unequal opportunity. The earlier classroom was neither pure nor worthless. The present question is how quickly inherited arrangements are being unsettled, and how many people will take part in constructing workable new ones.

A workshop of short comics is a small contribution to that effort. Its value depends on what people notice, question, revise, and carry into the next piece of work. The mistakes are consequential without making the experiment a failure; the pleasures are real without proving that every use of AI is good. We can build something, examine what it did to our intentions, and make a more intelligent next attempt. Shared across many classrooms and workshops, those small acts of design can help education remain connected to where people's lives are headed.
