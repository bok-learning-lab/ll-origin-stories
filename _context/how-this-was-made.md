# How this collection was made

The comics began with a teaching activity, before there was a collection or a reusable skill. Marlon designed an origin-story exercise for TDM 155AI: start with something a person knows about their own experience, turn it into a short superhero story, and use the resulting images as material for conversation and reflection. During the week, Marlon, Madeleine, Jordan and Jonah helped lead versions of the activity with different groups. They became the cast of this experiment because they had helped run it. They are four workshop leaders, not a complete roster of the Learning Lab.

On Friday morning, September 11, 2026, Marlon talked with Claude on his way to work. The conversation developed both the activity's educational rationale and the stories that might be told about the people helping lead it. What counted as a “superpower” in their work? Where had it come from? What changed when an ability formed in one environment entered another? Marlon's childhood interest in comics and academic interest in cultural history gave those questions another direction: different superhero traditions offered different explanations of a person's power.

The resulting briefing distinguished things Marlon had said from proposals he had accepted and questions still open. That distinction mattered. A threatened planet of writing teachers could be a useful fictional device without becoming a claim that Harvard's writing program had closed. Jonah's continuing work in Expos complicated the apparent escape story. Madeleine's folklore and botanical catalogs suggested meaningful structures that could become software. Jordan's arts-and-education background suggested a power that restored color to a world inclined to call creative work “fun” as a dismissal. The longer argument behind the exercise is developed in [Where the powers come from](why-origin-stories.md).

Marlon then worked with Astra to build and run a reusable [origin-story skill](../.agents/skills/origin-story/SKILL.md). The aim was to make the process available beyond this particular conversation. A user could bring a paragraph of context, answer a few targeted questions, choose a longer interview, or say to proceed with what was already available. The workflow would support four- or eight-panel stories, individual origins and team sequences, whole-page images and separately generated panels. Each project would keep its scripts and images together and provide a local reader.

The division of work was explicit. Marlon supplied the purpose, source experience, preferences and corrections. Astra wrote the panel-by-panel captions, dialogue and visual instructions. The native image-generation tool rendered those instructions into pictures. We did not use a separate text-generation API to write the captions, and the image tool's underlying model name was not exposed. Node utilities were also built for OpenRouter, fal and HUIT, but the finished native-tool images are not evidence that those separate API adapters were tested with live accounts.

The first attempts showed why writing before rendering was necessary and insufficient. A page could look accomplished while failing to establish its setting. Marlon's writing-program planet appeared in the art instructions, but the reader-facing words did not explain that its inhabitants taught writing, recognized patterns and helped people organize academic ideas. An overcompressed caption about a shorter “reel” was unclear, and a proposed explanation about being hired for video was wrong. Marlon corrected the account. The skill was revised to require checking what the visible captions actually told a new reader. Rectangular narration boxes became a way to give the story enough context to stand on its own.

Character design developed through a similar exchange. Photographs supplied likeness, but early sheets were more photographic than the desired comics. Marlon asked for dramatic, idealized comic versions, including visibly younger and older versions of himself. The sheets became continuity references rather than portraits to paste into every scene. Their original images were retained, and the workflow made compressed JPEG copies for subsequent generations. Keeping the reference small enough to reuse—at most 1,536 pixels along its longest edge and below 512 KiB—made it practical to attach the same character information repeatedly.

Jordan's setting needed another kind of correction. She was from Georgia, not Atlanta. More fundamentally, her origin should unfold on a magical ancient island where everyone was an artist. Hawaii's sea and mountains and rural Georgia's farms and horses were comparisons that helped readers imagine that place; they were not modern locations literally combined into a campus. Her opening page was revised while the successful second page and character sheet were preserved. Keeping earlier takes made the change inspectable instead of erasing the first interpretation.

Once the approach was established, three agents worked in parallel on Jordan, Jonah and Madeleine as separate projects, while the parent agent handled Marlon and the team assembly. Each project wrote its own exact text, generated a suitable sheet, passed the compressed reference into the page generations, and checked the resulting lettering and continuity. The parallel work saved time without making the characters interchangeable. Their different powers were also the basis of the assembly story: bringing people together should require what each could do.

The first complete collection contained fifteen stories: four individual origins and a team story in each of three editions. The immigrant version made familiar abilities extraordinary in a new world. The 1960s exposure version used an accident to create a new capacity. The dark mutant version made an innate difference emerge under pressure. New sheets changed the visual language along with the mechanism. Marlon subsequently asked for the mutant origins to expand to eight panels and for a fourth, comic resources parody about being extraordinarily well equipped. Those extensions are now complete: four expanded mutant origins and The Discretionary Avengers, an eight-panel parody in which the quartet’s emergency is a free-plan limit. That version of the index presented sixteen current comics, with the earlier short mutant versions still available.

The collection subsequently grew to thirty current eight-panel comics. Five printed Golden Age versions re-rendered the immigrant stories without changing their text. Four modern individual antihero stories extended the resources parody into luxury resorts, influencer culture, manufactured scarcity, and compulsory taste. A separate 1980s corporate-gothic edition inverted the same abilities: The Final Draft edits away other interpretations, The Double Bind makes refusal count as consent, The Closed Index preserves living things until they cannot change, and The Chromarch makes her taste compulsory. Each received an eight-panel origin showing a useful first application of the power and a deliberate choice to misuse it; the earlier villain ensemble remained as their team story. The modern and villain pages used native image generation, with exact scripts written first and edition-specific character references attached. Their fortunes, institutions, magical instruments, and wrongdoing are fictional. Each comic now has its own card and reader on the main index.

The reader gathers selected artwork; the production workflow preserves the account of making it. Both matter to this activity. The images offer something immediate to enjoy and discuss. The scripts, corrections and alternative origins make it possible to examine what the form introduced, what the agent misunderstood, and what the human author chose to keep.

Later on September 11, Marlon asked for the immigrant-edition stories to be made again with one change: the pictures should actually look like Golden Age comics. The five scripts kept every caption and line of dialogue, and only the visual direction was rewritten, asking for the printed object rather than a drawing style: yellowed newsprint, four-color letterpress with visible Ben-Day dots, misregistered flat primaries, thick uniform outlines, hand-lettered capitals, yellow caption boxes, finned rockets and electronic brains. This edition used the repository's OpenRouter route with Google's Gemini image model, so it is the first set of comics here made through the API adapters rather than a host's native tool. New character sheets were generated from the same reference photographs. The style arrived almost immediately; the lettering did not. The model repeatedly added a second row of invented panels, lettered art directions as captions, dropped a caption, or misspelled a word, and once it drew a famous chest emblem that had to be edited back out. Each page was read against its script and regenerated, with an explicit list of the only words allowed on the page, until the printed text matched the written one. Jordan's story uses the printing process itself for the gag: the Faculty of Arts and Sciences panels drop to black ink and gray dots, and color returns at the Lab.
